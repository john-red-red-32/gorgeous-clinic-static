Polynucleotides: The New Skill Gap in Regenerative Aesthetics, What polynucleotides are and why demand is rising

Polynucleotides are injectable regenerative products derived from purified DNA fragments that aim to support dermal biology rather than simply adding volume. Clinically they are used as skin boosters, to improve texture, tone and hydration, and in targeted areas such as under-eye rejuvenation and crepey necks. Patients often describe results as improved skin quality and resilience rather than an obvious volumetric change.
Interest has risen because many patients now prefer subtler, tissue-quality treatments after seeing the limitations of overfilling. That shift creates a real market opportunity for practitioners able to offer careful regenerative work alongside established filler and toxin services.

Who this may suit in clinic

Polynucleotide work suits practitioners who already have a firm grounding in facial anatomy, aseptic technique and complication recognition. If you can assess vascular risk zones, map lymphatic drainage and manage hyaluronic filler logistics, you are well placed to add regenerative injections. That said, these products require different assessment and patient expectation skills than HA filler.
Patients who benefit tend to be those seeking improved skin quality, early signs of ageing, textural irregularity or under-eye brightness, and those who want a gentler, maintenance-led pathway. For many clinics this complements injectable moisturisers and skin booster services, expanding your offer without competing directly with standard volumising filler sessions.

What beginner courses often miss

Many entry-level aesthetics courses focus on technique and basic product handling. They often gloss over product selection for regenerative lines, realistic expectation-setting, and subtleties of dilution, injection depth and sequencing that affect outcome. Equally important is training in patient selection and when to offer alternatives such as microneedling, PRP or topical regimens.
A responsible advanced course will include case-based planning, complication recognition for non-HA injectables, and hands-on supervised practice. It should also flag prescribing and governance requirements for your region, and how to integrate the service into existing consultations and pricing models [1].

Where hands-on training matters

Online modules can reliably teach the science, product differences and consultation structure. They are useful for background knowledge and CPD. However, supervised hands-on practice is non-negotiable for safe polynucleotide work. You need tactile experience with micro-droplet placements, cannula versus needle choices in specific zones, and live decision-making when tissue response differs from expectation.
Look for masterclasses with small student-to-tutor ratios, live models or simulated tissues, and clear escalation pathways for complications. Also check whether the provider covers aftercare sequencing and realistic review intervals so your first patients get safe, reproducible care. If you already offer skin boosters, adding polynucleotides is often a natural next step within your existing service offering [[LINK:injectable-moisturisers-1|injectable moisturisers and skin boosters]].

Choosing training and sensible next steps

Prioritise training that balances evidence, hands-on supervision and realistic commercial integration. Good signposts are clear learning outcomes, limited cohort sizes, tutor CVs that show credible clinical experience, and post-course mentoring or case review. Ask prospective providers how they address complications, product selection across skin types, and long-term maintenance plans.
