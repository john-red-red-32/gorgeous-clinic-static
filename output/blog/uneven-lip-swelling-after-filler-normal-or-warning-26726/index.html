Uneven Lip Swelling After Filler: Normal Reaction Or Warning Sign?

Jenna Hickson
5 minute read
Back to blog
You injected a patient, the lips look uneven and one side feels firmer: is this normal post-procedure swelling or a sign you missed something important? For practising injectors the job is to distinguish expected asymmetry from complications needing escalation. This piece gives a practical, clinician-focused framework to assess timing, distribution, associated signs and likely causes. It helps you know when conservative aftercare is reasonable, when to prescribe antivirals or antibiotics, and when to escalate urgently.

Uneven Lip Swelling After Filler: Normal Reaction Or Warning Sign?, What uneven swelling looks like

Early post-treatment asymmetry is common. Patients often report one side feeling firmer or looking larger within the first 24 to 72 hours. This can be simple oedema from tissue manipulation, a localised inflammatory response to the hyaluronic acid, or uneven diffusion related to anatomy and product placement.
Key distinguishing details are texture and associated signs. Soft, diffuse swelling without severe pain or skin changes favours normal oedema. Firm, focal swelling that increases over 24 hours, is tender, or shows skin colour change needs closer assessment. Ask about systemic symptoms, worsening pain, spreading redness, or fever when triaging.

Timeline: what to expect

Expect the peak of reactive swelling within 24 to 72 hours. Most uncomplicated lip filler swelling declines steadily over the first two weeks. Minor asymmetry can persist beyond this while the product settles and lymphatic drainage normalises.
If swelling begins immediately and reduces day by day, conservative measures are appropriate: ice in short intervals, head elevation, and clear aftercare advice. If the patient reports progressive swelling after 72 hours, worsening pain, or new systemic symptoms, escalate assessment and consider differential causes rather than assuming normal settling.

Common causes to consider

Bruising and haematoma can look like unilateral swelling, often with localised discolouration and focal firmness. Small vessel trauma is common around the lip vermillion and should be managed expectantly unless expanding rapidly.
Herpes simplex reactivation is a frequent mimic of focal lip swelling, usually with prodromal tingling and later grouped vesicles. Early antiviral treatment reduces severity and limits secondary bacterial infection. Low-grade bacterial infection is less common but possible, especially if asepsis was compromised or after repeated needling. Always check the injection history, antisepsis steps and any patient immunosuppression.

Vascular warning signs to recognise

Vascular compromise is uncommon but urgent. Look for severe pain out of proportion to the procedure, pallor or mottling of the lip, livedo reticularis, delayed capillary refill, or persistent numbness. These signs can appear immediately or develop within hours. Blanching around the injection site that does not rebound is an important early red flag.
If vascular occlusion is suspected act quickly: stop injecting, give high-flow warm measures, massage in appropriate cases, and arrange immediate review with hyaluronidase available according to your local protocol. Document findings with photos and timed notes. When in doubt about vascular injury escalate to an experienced colleague or a local emergency pathway without delay.
1
Dermal filler vascular complications - MHRA (https://www.gov.uk) - 2023

Practical assessment, documentation and next steps

Begin with a structured history and focused exam. Note timing, symptom progression, pain quality, systemic signs, any vesicles, and whether bruising is present. Photograph in consistent lighting and angles immediately, and again at follow-up. Clear, timed records protect the patient and practitioner.
Treatment choices depend on the likely cause: conservative aftercare for simple oedema, antivirals for suspected herpes, antibiotics when bacterial infection is likely, and prompt hyaluronidase for vascular occlusion according to your clinic protocol.
